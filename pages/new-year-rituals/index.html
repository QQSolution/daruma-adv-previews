5 New Year Rituals Worth Keeping in 2027 (and the One You Can See Every Day)

Grapes at midnight, a letter to yourself, one word for the year. We looked at 5 rituals people around the world actually keep, and what each one is really for.

By the Daruma Ritual team · 6 min read

Resolutions have a reputation problem. Most of us make them on January 1st and forget them before the decorations are back in the attic.

Rituals are different. A ritual is something you do, at a set moment, often with other people. That's why some of them have lasted for centuries.

So instead of another list of goals, here are 5 New Year rituals worth keeping, what each one is good for, and the one we think works best if you want the goal to still be in the room with you in March.

Why most resolutions fade

It usually isn't willpower. A resolution is a sentence you say once, on a night when you're tired and happy, and then it has nowhere to live. By the third week of January it's competing with work, school runs and the weather, and the sentence loses.

The rituals that last give the year a moment, and give the goal a place. Here's how 5 of them do it.

What we looked for

  • Takes minutes, not hours
  • Works alone or with family
  • Gives the year a clear starting moment
  • Still means something after January

1. The 12 grapes at midnight

In Spain, people eat one grape on each of the 12 strokes of midnight, one for every month ahead. It's chaotic and very funny, and it's a lovely way to start a year with the people you love.

Good for: a joyful, shared start. Less good for: anything you need to remember in February.

2. A letter to yourself

Write a letter to the person you'll be on December 31st: what you hope happened, what you're worried about, what you want to remember. Seal it and open it a year later.

Good for: reflection, and a wonderful moment next December. Less good for: it lives in a drawer for 12 months.

3. One word for the year

Instead of a list, pick a single word: "steady", "brave", "home". Put it somewhere you'll see it.

Good for: a mood for the year. Less good for: a word is a direction, not a finish line.

4. The first walk of the year

Plenty of families start the year with a walk on the morning of January 1st: a beach, a park, a hill with a view. Many talk about the year ahead as they go.

Good for: a clear head and good conversation. Less good for: the conversation is over by lunch.

5. The Daruma: one goal, one painted eye

This ritual comes from Japan and is around 400 years old. A Daruma is a small round doll that arrives with both eyes blank. You choose one goal, paint one eye, and keep it somewhere you'll see it every day. When the goal is done, you paint the second eye.

It's the ritual that does the one thing the others don't: it stays in the room with you. You see it while you drink your coffee, while you answer email, while you're deciding whether to do the thing today.

It's also weighted at the base, so it rocks back up when you push it over. That's where the Japanese proverb comes from: fall seven times, rise eight.

Why we'd keep the Daruma, even if you keep the others

These rituals aren't competitors. Eat the grapes. Write the letter. Take the walk. They're all lovely ways to start a year.

But if you want one goal to survive past February, the Daruma is the one built for it:

  • It asks for one goal, not a list
  • You see it every day, not once a year
  • It has a finish line, the second eye
  • It's a gift you can give to everyone at the table
  1. Day 1Choose one goal. Write it down and paint the first eye: the doll's own left, which is the one on your right as it faces you. Any black marker works (one isn't in the box).
  2. Every day afterThe Daruma sits where you'll see it: a desk, a shelf, a nightstand. One eye open, waiting.
  3. Week 4Look at it and ask yourself one honest question: what did I do this week toward the goal?
  4. The day it's donePaint the second eye. Keep it as a record, or start again with a new goal.
